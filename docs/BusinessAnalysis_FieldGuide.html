Directing, Evaluating, and Approving AI-Generated Requirements

You will not write every requirement by hand. An AI assistant will draft most of the first pass. Your job as an AI Solution Architect is the ~20% that keeps that draft honest: knowing what a requirement must contain, spotting the ones that are vague or untestable, and refusing to approve anything that cannot be verified. This guide teaches that 20% using one fully worked example â€” an insurance claims-triage initiative â€” so every concept below is grounded in a real, internally consistent set of documents rather than abstract advice.

Why BA Exists

Every failed software project traces back to one of two root causes: the team built the wrong thing correctly, or the right thing incorrectly. Business analysis exists to prevent the first failure. It is the discipline of translating a fuzzy business need â€” "claims take too long," "customers are churning," "we're losing money to fraud" â€” into a specification precise enough that a developer, or an AI code generator, cannot reasonably misinterpret it.

In an AI-accelerated delivery model, this discipline becomes more important, not less. A large language model will confidently generate a complete, plausible-looking requirements document from a two-sentence prompt. It will invent acceptance criteria, invent priorities, and invent scope boundaries â€” all fluently, and some of it wrong. The architect who cannot tell a well-formed requirement from a plausible-sounding one will approve those inventions into production.

The core riskAI-generated requirements fail silently. They read well. They are formatted correctly. The defect is not in the prose â€” it is in what the prose fails to specify. Your review lens has to catch omission, not just error.

The 20% You Need

You do not need to become a full-time business analyst. You need fluency in seven building blocks, enough to direct one, evaluate one, and reject one that's broken.

Good vs. Bad Requirements

This is the single highest-leverage skill in this guide. AI models are fluent enough to produce requirements that look complete while being functionally useless. Train your eye on the pattern below.

Bad â€” vague, untestable

"The system should process claims quickly and be secure."

No actor, no number, no acceptance test possible. "Quickly" and "secure" mean nothing to a QA engineer or an auditor.

Good â€” specific, testable

"The system shall return an AI triage recommendation within 90 seconds (p95) of document upload for 95% of submitted claims. All PII fields shall be encrypted at rest using AES-256."

Two atomic requirements, each with a number, a threshold, and an owner who can verify it.

Bad â€” solutioning disguised as a requirement

"Build a microservice using a vector database to search claims."

This specifies an implementation, not a need. It removes the architect's ability to choose a better solution later.

Good â€” need, not implementation

"Adjusters shall be able to retrieve the 5 most similar historical claims to a given claim, ranked by relevance, within 3 seconds."

States the need and the measurable bar. How it's built is an architecture decision, made later, deliberately.

Red flag checklistReject or return any requirement that: (1) has no verb a tester can act on, (2) uses an unquantified adverb ("fast," "easy," "robust"), (3) bundles two behaviors into one requirement ID, or (4) has no stated priority.

The Architect's Review Lens

When an AI assistant hands you a requirements artifact, run it through five questions before you approve anything downstream of it:

  1. Is it atomic? One requirement, one testable behavior. Split anything joined by "and" that describes two different outcomes.
  2. Is it prioritized honestly? A MoSCoW list that is 100% Must has not been prioritized â€” it has been avoided. Expect friction and pushback here; that's a sign prioritization happened.
  3. Is it traceable? Every requirement should point to a business objective above it and a test case below it. No orphans in either direction.
  4. Does it define failure, not just success? A use case or story with only a happy path is half a specification. Ask explicitly: what happens when this fails, times out, or receives bad input?
  5. Is the non-functional bar explicit? Performance, security, and compliance targets must carry numbers an auditor could verify without asking the author what they meant.
Approval disciplineApproving a document is a governance act, not a courtesy. Sign your name to the sign-off block only once every requirement in it has passed this five-question lens.

The Problem

Meridian's average claim takes 12.4 days to close, driven by manual document review, ad-hoc fraud checks, and an average of 1.4 missing-document round trips per claim. Only 8% of claims qualify for straight-through processing today. Undetected fraud indicators cost an estimated $6.8M annually. Adjusters each carry an average open caseload of 145 claims, well above the 90-claim caseload at which quality holds steady per Meridian's own 2025 quality audit.

The Recommendation

Fund ClaimSight AI: an AI-assisted intake, extraction, and triage layer in front of the existing claims management system. The system extracts structured data from submitted documents and photos, computes a severity and fraud-risk score, and either routes a claim to straight-through payout (low risk, high confidence) or to an adjuster's queue with a pre-filled recommendation the adjuster can accept or override. Every automated decision is logged with inputs, model version, and rationale for audit.

1. Purpose & Scope

This BRD specifies the business requirements for ClaimSight AI, an AI-assisted intake, extraction, triage, and fraud-scoring layer for Meridian Mutual Insurance's Auto, Property, Liability, and Comprehensive claim lines. It governs claim intake through routing and decision-logging; it does not govern claims payment processing itself, which remains the responsibility of the existing Claims Management System (out of scope, see §5).

2. Background

Baseline metrics (trailing 12 months, all lines): average cycle time 12.4 days; straight-through processing rate 8%; annual fraud loss $6.8M; average cost per claim $145; average adjuster open caseload 145; claims CSAT 68/100. Annual volume is approximately 186,000 claims (Auto 58%, Property 27%, Liability 11%, Comprehensive/Other 4%). See the Vision & Business Case for the full financial rationale.

3. In Scope

  • Digital claim intake (web and mobile) with guided document/photo upload.
  • AI-based structured data extraction from submitted documents and photos.
  • AI severity and fraud-risk scoring with explainable contributing factors.
  • Automated routing: straight-through payout for low-risk/high-confidence claims; adjuster triage queue for all others.
  • Adjuster override capability with mandatory reason capture.
  • SIU notification workflow for high fraud-risk claims.
  • Full audit logging of every AI-influenced decision.

6. Assumptions & Constraints

Five core use cases, each with main flow, alternate flows, and exception flows.

UC-01 — Submit New Claim

Actor: Policyholder · Trigger: A covered loss occurs · Preconditions: Active policy on file.

Main flow:

  1. Policyholder logs into the portal and selects "File a new claim."
  2. Policyholder enters loss details (date, location, type) and uploads supporting documents/photos.
  3. System (FR-03) extracts structured data in real time and displays it for confirmation.
  4. System (FR-04) checks document completeness against the claim type's required list.
  5. Policyholder submits; system generates a claim ID and starts the AI triage process (UC-02).

Alternate flow A1 — Missing documents: At step 4, if required documents are missing, the system lists the specific missing items and lets the policyholder either upload them now or submit with a flagged "pending documents" status; a follow-up reminder is scheduled (FR-10).

Exception flow E1 — Portal unavailable: If the portal cannot be reached, the policyholder is directed to phone intake; the phone agent enters the claim manually into the same intake schema so downstream AI triage (UC-02) still runs.

Postconditions: A claim record exists with a unique ID and an intake timestamp that starts the cycle-time clock.

UC-01 Sequence

UC-02 — AI Triage & Fraud Scoring

Actor: System (ClaimSight AI), on behalf of Claims Operations · Trigger: A claim record is created (UC-01 postcondition).

Main flow: System computes severity and fraud-risk scores (FR-05), attaches the top 3 contributing factors (FR-06), and routes the claim (FR-07): high-confidence/low-risk → straight-through payout; all else → adjuster triage queue.

Alternate flow A1 — Borderline confidence: If model confidence falls in the borderline band, the claim routes to the adjuster queue regardless of risk score — confidence, not just risk, gates straight-through eligibility.

Exception flow E1 — Low-confidence extraction: If document extraction (FR-03) confidence is below threshold, the claim routes directly to manual data entry by an intake clerk before scoring proceeds; the AI does not score on unreliable extracted data.

Postconditions: Every claim carries a severity score, a fraud-risk score, a routing decision, and a logged rationale (FR-11) before any human touches it.

UC-03 — Adjuster Reviews AI Recommendation

Actor: Claims Adjuster · Trigger: A claim lands in the adjuster's triage queue.

Main flow: Adjuster opens the claim, reviews the AI-extracted data and recommendation, and accepts it, closing the claim per the recommended action.

Alternate flow A1 — Override: Adjuster disagrees with the AI recommendation, selects "Override," and must enter a reason of at least 10 characters (FR-08) before the override saves; the override and reason are logged (FR-11) and feed model-quality monitoring.

UC-04 — SIU Investigates Flagged Claim

Actor: SIU Fraud Analyst · Trigger: Automated SIU notification (FR-09) on a high fraud-risk score.

Main flow: Analyst reviews the claim, the fraud score, and its top 3 contributing factors; opens a formal investigation if warranted; documents findings.

Alternate flow A1 — False positive: Analyst determines the flag was a false positive, closes the investigation with a documented reason, and the claim returns to normal adjuster workflow (UC-03).

Exception flow E1 — Insufficient evidence: If evidence is inconclusive within the investigation SLA, the analyst escalates to Legal for a formal demand for additional documentation from the policyholder, and the claim's payout is held pending that response.

UC-05 — Compliance Audit of an Automated Decision

Actor: Compliance & Regulatory Affairs Officer · Trigger: Regulator inquiry or scheduled internal audit.

Main flow: Officer retrieves the full decision log for a claim (FR-11): inputs at time of decision, model version, computed scores, contributing factors, and routing outcome. Officer reconstructs the decision without needing an engineer, satisfying NFR-04 and NFR-05.
